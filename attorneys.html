Utah's No-Fault Law & Vehicle Property Damage Claims

Utah's Motor Vehicle No-Fault Law (Utah Statutes §§ 627.730–627.7405) requires all drivers to carry $10,000 in Personal Injury Protection (PIP) coverage. PIP pays medical expenses and lost wages for the insured, regardless of fault — but it governs bodily injury only. Property damage claims, including diminished value, are entirely separate and operate under traditional tort principles.

Utah courts have consistently held that vehicle owners are entitled to recover the full extent of property damage caused by an at-fault driver, including:

  • Cost of repair or restoration — The reasonable cost to return the vehicle to its pre-loss condition
  • Diminished value — The difference between pre-accident market value and post-repair market value, even after complete repairs
  • Loss of use — Compensation for the period the vehicle was unavailable during repairs

Under O.C.G.A. § 9-3-32, clients generally have four years from the date of the accident to bring a property damage claim — giving attorneys adequate time to document and pursue the full scope of vehicle-related damages on behalf of accident victims.

Utah Diminished Value Law — Key Points for Attorneys

Utah law strongly favors diminished value recovery. Here is a summary of the controlling law:

  • No-Fault Scope is Limited: Utah is a tort state (it repealed no-fault in 1991). All property damage claims — including diminished value — are recoverable from the at-fault driver’s liability insurer under traditional tort principles. The Utah Supreme Court’s landmark ruling in Allstate v. Hawkins (2001) also established inherent diminished value recovery under first-party collision coverage.
  • Third-Party DV Claims are Recognized: Utah case law and the Utah Restatement of Torts recognize the right to recover the difference between pre-accident fair market value and post-repair fair market value as a component of property damages. This is commonly referred to as "inherent diminished value."
  • Statute of Limitations: Property damage claims are governed by the four-year statute under O.C.G.A. § 9-3-32, running from the date of the accident.
  • First-Party vs. Third-Party: First-party DV claims against your own comprehensive/collision insurer are generally not available unless specifically provided by policy. Third-party DV claims against the at-fault driver's liability insurer are fully available.
  • Documentation Required: Courts and insurers require a certified, documented appraisal to prove DV — bare assertions of value loss are insufficient. An ASCAA-certified, USPAP-compliant appraisal from Salt Lake City Auto Appraisers provides the evidentiary foundation your case requires.

This summary is provided as general information for attorneys and does not constitute legal advice. Laws change; attorneys should verify current statutes and case law applicable to each matter.
